Characteristic radiation is easier to understand when you picture an atom trying to repair an empty space in one of its inner electron shells.

Start with a tungsten atom in the anode target. Its electrons occupy shells with specific binding energies. Electrons in the inner shells are held much more tightly than electrons farther from the nucleus.

Now imagine a high-speed projectile electron arriving from the cathode. If that projectile electron has enough kinetic energy, it can collide with an inner-shell electron and eject it completely from the tungsten atom. The atom has now been ionized, and there is a vacancy in the inner shell.

That vacancy makes the atom unstable. An electron from a higher-energy shell can drop down to fill the empty space. When it drops from the higher shell to the lower shell, it loses energy. That energy is released as an X-ray photon.

The important part is that the photon energy is not random. It equals the difference between the binding energy of the higher shell and the binding energy of the lower shell. Because those binding energies are specific to tungsten, the emitted photon has a specific energy that is characteristic of tungsten. That is where the name characteristic radiation comes from.

This is very different from Bremsstrahlung radiation. Bremsstrahlung happens when a projectile electron is slowed or deflected near the nucleus, producing photons with a continuous range of energies. Characteristic radiation depends on electron-shell vacancies and transitions, so it produces discrete photon energies.

There is also an energy threshold. The projectile electron must have at least enough energy to remove the inner-shell electron. If it does not have enough energy to overcome the binding energy of that shell, that particular characteristic interaction cannot occur.

Clinical connection: the target material matters. Tungsten is used because its atomic structure, high atomic number, and heat tolerance make it effective for diagnostic X-ray production. The characteristic photon energies are tied to tungsten's electron binding energies.

Registry trap: do not confuse the energy of the projectile electron with the final characteristic photon energy. The characteristic photon energy equals the difference between shell binding energies, not simply the full kinetic energy of the incoming electron.

Make it click: first create an inner-shell vacancy, then let an outer electron fall into it, then release the energy difference as a specific X-ray photon. Vacancy, transition, photon. That three-step picture is the heart of characteristic radiation.
