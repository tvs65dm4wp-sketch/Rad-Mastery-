The inverse square law is one of the most useful radiation safety concepts because it explains why even a small increase in distance can dramatically reduce exposure.

The law describes how radiation intensity changes as distance from a point source changes. Radiation spreads out as it travels. Imagine the same number of photons spreading over the surface of a larger and larger sphere. As the radius of that sphere increases, its surface area increases with the square of the distance.

That is why intensity is inversely proportional to distance squared.

Here is the practical relationship: intensity one divided by intensity two equals distance two squared divided by distance one squared.

But you do not have to memorize it as a random fraction. Start with the idea that doubling your distance does not cut exposure in half. It reduces exposure to one fourth. Tripling your distance reduces exposure to one ninth.

For example, imagine a technologist receives a scatter exposure rate of 4 milligray per hour at one meter from a source. If the technologist moves to two meters, the new exposure rate is 1 milligray per hour. The distance doubled, so intensity became one fourth.

Now try three meters. Relative to one meter, distance increased by a factor of three. Three squared is nine, so intensity becomes one ninth of the original.

This is why distance is such a powerful occupational protection tool during portable radiography and fluoroscopy. If you can safely take even a few steps away from the source and patient, your exposure can drop substantially.

Clinical connection: during a mobile exposure, maximize distance from the patient and X-ray source while maintaining control of the equipment and patient safety. During fluoroscopy, use distance together with shielding and good C-arm geometry.

Common mistake: the inverse square law works best for a point source in free space. Real clinical environments include scatter, shielding, and complex geometry, so measured exposure may not be perfectly ideal. But the principle remains extremely valuable.

Registry trap: if distance doubles, intensity becomes one fourth, not one half. If distance is cut in half, intensity becomes four times greater.

Make it click: distance changes in a straight line, but intensity changes with the square. That is why stepping farther away is more powerful than it first seems.
